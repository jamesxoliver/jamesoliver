The Buildable Sphere

The sphere encloses a volume with the least surface, a sphere cannot be built from flat parts, and geodesic domes approach it without limit.

A soap bubble is a sphere. Its film pulls inward until it holds the air with the least surface it can, and among all closed surfaces holding a given volume, the sphere has the least area. A builder who wants to enclose a room with the least material faces the same problem and cannot use the same answer, because a builder works with flat panels and a sphere has no flat part. The question is how close a structure built from flat parts can come to the sphere.

We assume that the material in a thin shell is proportional to its area. For a shell of density ρ\rho and thickness tt the mass is M=ρtSM=\rho\,t\,S, so enclosing a volume with the least material is the same problem as enclosing it with the least surface. We also assume that a buildable structure is made of flat parts joined at finite precision, and that it must hold its shape with pinned joints.

The first assumption turns a structural question into a geometric one, and the isoperimetric inequality answers it: among all closed surfaces enclosing a volume VV, the sphere alone has the least area. For a sphere of radius rr,

Ssphere=4πr2,Vsphere=43πr3.S_{\mathrm{sphere}}=4\pi r^2,\qquad V_{\mathrm{sphere}}=\tfrac{4}{3}\pi r^3.

The same answer covers a dome standing on the ground. Reflected in the ground plane, a dome becomes a closed surface with twice its volume and twice its built area, so the hemisphere is the enclosure of least area that stands on a plane.

The second assumption rules the sphere out. A surface made of flat parts is a polyhedron, and a polyhedron enclosing VV has more surface than the sphere enclosing VV. The minimum exists, and no buildable structure reaches it. What remains to ask is whether buildable structures can come arbitrarily close.

To hold its shape with pinned joints, a structure needs triangular faces. A triangle cannot change shape without changing the length of a side, while a face with four or more sides can fold at its corners unless its joints resist bending. A convex polyhedron whose faces are all triangles is rigid to first order.

Definition 1. Inscribe a regular icosahedron in a sphere, divide each of its faces into ν2\nu^2 triangles, and project every new vertex outward onto the sphere. The resulting polyhedron 𝒫ν\mathcal{P}_\nu is the geodesic dome of frequency ν\nu.

Each 𝒫ν\mathcal{P}_\nu is convex and every face is a flat triangle, so each is buildable and rigid. As the frequency rises the vertices crowd the sphere, and

S(𝒫ν)→Ssphere,V(𝒫ν)→Vsphere(ν→∞).S(\mathcal{P}_\nu)\to S_{\mathrm{sphere}},\qquad V(\mathcal{P}_\nu)\to V_{\mathrm{sphere}}\qquad(\nu\to\infty).

Theorem 1. Among enclosures of volume VV built from flat parts, no surface area is as small as SsphereS_{\mathrm{sphere}}, and geodesic domes come arbitrarily close to it. For any ε>0\varepsilon>0 there is a frequency ν\nu at which the geodesic dome of volume VV has surface area at most Ssphere+εS_{\mathrm{sphere}}+\varepsilon.

Proof. Every enclosure built from flat parts is a polyhedron, and by the isoperimetric inequality its area exceeds that of the sphere of the same volume. For the approach, scale each 𝒫ν\mathcal{P}_\nu so that it encloses exactly VV. Since V(𝒫ν)→VsphereV(\mathcal{P}_\nu)\to V_{\mathrm{sphere}}, the scale factor tends to one, and the scaled areas tend to SsphereS_{\mathrm{sphere}}. Scaling keeps every face flat and triangular, so each scaled dome is still buildable and rigid. ▫\square

Corollary 1. A hemispherical dome on the ground covers a volume VV with a surface of (18π)1/3V2/3≈3.84V2/3(18\pi)^{1/3}V^{2/3}\approx 3.84\,V^{2/3}. A cube on the ground covers the same volume with walls and a roof of 5V2/35\,V^{2/3}. The dome uses 23 percent less surface, and a geodesic dome approaches that figure as its frequency rises.

Corollary 2. The surface by which a geodesic dome exceeds the sphere of equal volume falls in inverse proportion to its number of joints. With NN joints the excess is close to 0.62/N0.62/N of the sphere’s area, so halving the excess doubles the joints.

Proof. Each flat triangle lies below the sphere by a distance proportional to the square of its edge, and the edge shrinks in proportion to 1/ν1/\nu, so the excess area falls as 1/ν21/\nu^2. A geodesic sphere of frequency ν\nu has 10ν2+210\nu^2+2 joints, which grow as ν2\nu^2. The product of the excess and the number of joints therefore settles to a constant. Computed from Definition 1, it is 0.775, 0.671, 0.632, 0.623, 0.621 and 0.620 at frequencies 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 and 32. ▫\square

The theorem concerns surface area and nothing else. Geodesic domes are one route to the minimum, since any family of convex triangulated polyhedra that converges to the sphere approaches it too. The theorem says nothing about strength under load or about what a joint or a distinct panel costs to make, and the number of distinct panels grows with frequency as well.

The theorem would be false if a structure built from flat parts enclosed a volume with less surface than the sphere, which would contradict the isoperimetric inequality, or if the areas of geodesic domes failed to converge to the sphere’s, which can be checked by direct computation at any frequency.

The bubble reaches the sphere at once because nothing in it is flat. The dome reaches it by subdivision, and the gap can be made smaller than any tolerance named in advance.
